From Leadership to Learning,and on to Impact

2026/10/21 Hikaru
05
LeadershipLearningImpact
← ALL POSTS

To open with the point: leadership—keeping work moving steadily, coordinating it, leading and bringing talented people together, learning from one another, combining our strengths, and making complicated things happen—is something AI cannot currently replace.

When doing research, we care about whether a question is interesting, whether the method holds up, whether the experiments are solid, and whether we have produced a valuable result. Research itself involves plenty of uncertainty, but at least we roughly know what to work toward. Of course, AI can now solo quite a few things, too. But when we start leading a team and take responsibility for keeping something running and moving forward, simply handing it to AI often does not go so smoothly.

Leadership

TLDR↓

① Keeping things moving smoothly requires an overall plan and a tree of questions. You need to work through them and get them clear, and see things from other people's perspectives.

② Stay grounded when doing things. Don't lose your footing. Day-to-day team management and handling public reactions both deserve serious learning.

③ Especially with something this complicated, don't try to have AI finish it in one shot. Everything will be more complicated than you think. Try to work through every detail.

④ We also need to think about how to build a good workflow and clarify things early, instead of getting stuck in troubleshooting and polish: first, how to polish this thing well; second, when to call it finished.

The setting was this AI club competition. We needed an official website, a Wiki, a getting-started guide, competition tasks, and a competition platform. We needed to find people, divide up the work, coordinate progress, handle participants' opinions, and think about rules, fairness, and everyone's experience. At first I thought: great, the tasks are out, just rush them with AI. Luckily, I did put a team together. We were mostly doing our own things, but at least we got about 60% of the work done. I thought we were ready to release. Then, in a meeting, we got grilled on two things: first, presentation; second, how had we not even validated the competition tasks end to end? When asked, our answer was that we'd had AI run through them once.

Suddenly, there were so many things to think about. Is this score reasonable? Can this dataset work? Is this Docker setup appropriate? Are the task descriptions and scoring rules expressed properly? What happens when rules and personal relationships collide? If someone is unhappy, should we explain publicly or talk privately? The original plan needs adjusting, but the adjustment will affect other work—what then? This is complicated because you have to consider how multiple parties feel, alongside the complexity and soundness of the system itself. Some things have to be good enough, and some things cannot be polished indefinitely. In the end, we mobilized the whole team and managed to bring it all together steadily. It is a tree of questions that keeps recursing.

You can probably tell that I'm still very junior at these things. Older students taught me a lot of it hands-on: how to steady the situation, handle communication, and keep the overall work moving.

Somehow, though, this tells me two things. First, I still have a lot to learn. This feels difficult and painful, which means it is beyond my current ability. Second, this level of complexity is still beyond something AI can solve in one shot. Humans still have things worth doing. Thinking through a few of these points, the first is that, as a leader, doing things involves more than completing your own part well. You also need to understand what stage the whole project is at, what comes next, which problems deserve time, and how to help different people get things done together. When uncertainty appears, you need to make your own judgments and decisions, stay grounded, and not lose your footing.

The second is that, as a leader, you need to understand both your team and yourself. You need to know who is good at what, who fits which responsibility, how to coordinate different ideas, and how to handle conflicts of interest. Sometimes a decision itself is fine, but communicating it badly can make things much harder.

For example, on our team, some people are technically strong, some are good at engineering, some are especially attentive to writing and expression, and some are excellent at execution. I don't necessarily outperform them in every area, but we can organize our different abilities and accomplish something that would be difficult for one person to do alone.

As for myself, I tend to put myself through the wringer. Why am I handing out work? Why are people willing to work with me? If I assign something, do I at least need to understand it and do it better than the person I assign it to? I put someone in the right place, and they do it better than I could. And after meeting so many great people applying for Fall 2029, I wonder: am I a good researcher? Could I somehow be the worst of them? Am I just a router, someone who knows impressive people, knows who fits what, and connects them? I still haven't really figured this out. But I choose to give things time to bloom, stay grounded, do my own work well, bring everyone together, combine our strengths, and make myself worth it.

Learning

TLDR↓

① You can start now. You can learn by doing. You don't have to wait until you're ready.

② Learning has no fixed path. The sea is wide enough for fish to leap; the sky is high enough for birds to fly.

③ Between an accessible introduction and mastery, perhaps you don't always need to derive everything by hand or do it the old-fashioned way.

Our education often emphasizes this: want to work in communications? Sure, study signal processing—but first get fluent at deriving Fourier transforms by hand. Haha, you want to do integrated circuits too? Study electronics—but first hand-solder a circuit board and get the whole circuit working. Heard you want to work on frontier AI? Right, let me tell you: spend your first year learning mathematics, and in your second you can study computing. You need university physics, computer organization and architecture, and even a metalworking placement to file a hammer. In your third year I'll start teaching you machine learning and deep learning. Once you've finished all of those, I'll offer you a frontier-topics course in your fourth year. Finish that and: congratulations, you are finally allowed to start learning AI and doing research. By then, AI may already have run you over like a speed bump.

The first thing to think about is what counts as having learned something. I can derive it by hand, do it the old-fashioned way, and score very highly—does that mean I've learned it? Maybe not. Then I watch a crash course and have one round of conversation with AI. Have I learned it? Again, it can just look as though I have. Another question is the relationship between understanding something and being able to start doing it. Someone who wants to study computing certainly needs to understand programs, algorithms, and systems. But must they finish learning every underlying concept before they can start writing a useful program?

I prefer to see learning as a top-down process of learning by doing, moving back and forth: do something first, discover what you don't understand, and then learn it; learn something new, then return and improve your approach. As the questions deepen, the knowledge you need to master deepens too.

Some people prefer to study systematically before starting to practice, and that's fine too. We don't need to replace one way of learning with another. What deserves reflection is why one learning path gets treated as the only choice for everyone.

This is also what we hope to achieve by organizing the Wiki and preparing the getting-started guide. We don't want to give new students another syllabus they must complete from beginning to end. We want to offer a map: what directions exist, what resources are available, where you can begin, and where you can look for answers when you get stuck.

You can start with a direction that interests you, make a small project, or read a paper you actually want to understand. As you work, if you discover that you need more mathematics, go learn it. If you need to understand an underlying mechanism, keep digging. You don't need to feel that you cannot start now just because you haven't finished all the fundamentals.

Impact

TLDR:

① The AI club's value is to be everyone's interface with the frontier. We want people to grow and find what they actually want to do, beyond just reward hacking, and make real contributions to the community.

② There are more and more young, talented people. Our conclusion is that everyone has the right to develop freely. We hope evaluation systems and public opinion can recognize that too. What matters is how we can combine our strengths.

Writing this, I come back to why we started the AI club, built the Wiki, and organized the Wujie Cup in the first place.

For us, this is certainly a process of learning and growing. We have met many excellent peers, learned to organize projects, and gained experience through actual work. For everyone else, we have also created opportunities to learn and practice: putting together a getting-started guide so someone new can take their first step; holding the Wujie Cup so people can actually build projects; inviting people from different schools and fields into the AI club so those who were scattered can get to know one another, exchange ideas, and collaborate. You could even look at it this way: I could make this a competition where entering gets you 3k. But what would that mean? At the end of the day, thirty thousand yuan just makes a little noise. We want to get the younger crowd's enthusiasm and initiative going and have them work with us. Once the more outstanding newcomers emerge, we can further encourage those starting from a blank slate, so everyone can actually learn and figure out how they feel about this work. We want people to realize that age doesn't matter; what matters is to start now.

I also get the sense that there are more and more young, talented people, or outstanding juniors. We've met a group applying for Fall 2029, from different schools and fields. Some do research, some are good at engineering, some work on AI4S, some have strong execution and organizational skills, and some are starting businesses. We gradually meet through different channels, then come together around shared interests. Maybe this means AI is powerful and is helping us grow faster.

We've also seen plenty of hype—another young genius somewhere—or criticism: undergraduates should go back to being undergraduates; let's stop talking about AI; you should feel ashamed of stepping into society and working at the frontier, because you're a scab, because you're undermining fairness.

Putting the so-called hype aside, people are simply doing their thing at their own age. What is the point of selling age anxiety? Every "should" deserves to go. These are ordinary people doing things.

But anyway, since we're young, let's rush into it. Be crazy, be active. When we come together, we can combine our strengths, learn from one another, and do things worth doing.
